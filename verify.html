How it works

The company’s books are a Merkle tree — the design behind Certificate Transparency (RFC 9162), which every web browser relies on for its certificates. Each entry is hashed; pairs of hashes are hashed together, up to one hash at the top, the root. Change any entry, anywhere, and the root changes. The company’s Finance agent signs each root; its Audit agent rebuilds the books on its own device and co-signs.

  1. The checkpoint’s own signature.The device that sent it signed the whole file. One changed byte and that signature fails.
  2. The tree head inside it.Not a copy of a number: the exact write the Finance agent’s key signed when it sealed the books, checked against that key — and each Audit agent co-signature, checked against its own.
  3. The proof.An entry’s path to the signed root, or the path from your old root to the new one — recomputed here, hash by hash.
  4. Against what you hold.The new tree head must be signed by the same key as your saved checkpoint. Two signed checkpoints of the same size with different roots are not an error message: they are the proof the company signed two versions of its books.

What a verdict rests on

  • Proven by the file alone: who signed it, that not a byte has changed since, and that an entry is in the books the company signed — at that position, with those accounts and amounts.
  • Proven against your saved checkpoint: that the books only grew since you saved it. A company cannot quietly rewrite anything you have already been shown.
  • The keys: the first checkpoint you receive tells you the company’s signing keys. Confirm the sequencer key with the company once, by a channel of your own; every later file must carry the same key, and the checker refuses one that does not.
  • Comparing notes: a company could show two parties two different books. If you and its bank compare checkpoints of the same size, any difference is signed evidence of it.
  • What it does not prove: that an entry is true to the world — that the goods were delivered or the payment made. It proves what the company recorded, and that the record was never changed. That is the part an audit could not check before without trusting the company’s systems.

Why a transparency log, not a blockchain

One company does not need strangers to agree on its books; it needs anyone it shows them to — an auditor, a bank, a regulator — to be able to check two things on their own: that a given entry is in the books, and that the books were never rewritten. That is exactly what a transparency log gives, and it is what Certificate Transparency has done at internet scale since 2013: an append-only Merkle tree, a signed root, an inclusion proof, a consistency proof.

Sovereign Enterprise seals every posting into such a log as it happens. The Finance agent sequences and signs; the Audit agent rebuilds the books on a separate device and co-signs. Neither step asks you to trust them: the keys sign, the hashes add up or they do not, and the checkpoint you saved last quarter pins everything before it. The whole check is a few hundred lines you can read, running in your browser.
